Subject: Why we shouldn't let an LLM do the maths on trading data

Hi all,

I had been thinking  about LLM use and did some reasearch.  I also remembered some things I read while doing my masters.  And no, this text is not AI generated. 
I think it's worth being explicit about: we should not be asking an LLM to directly compute or analyse numerical data out of spreadsheets -  e.g trade files The AI absolutely has a role here, just not as the calculator.

Here's the short version of why.

An LLM doesn't actually calculate anything. It predicts  (uses probability) the next most likely token based on patterns in its training data. When it "sums" a column of 40,000 trades, it isn't running SUM() - it's generating text that looks like a plausible answer. Most of the time it'll be close. Close is fine for a chatbot recommending restaurants. It's not fine for a number that ends up in a  pack or gets reconciled against.



A few things fall out of that:

- Same file, same prompt, run twice - you can get two different answers. There's no guarantee of reproducibility, which is a problem the moment someone asks "can you re-run last month's numbers and confirm they match."
- There's no audit trail. If a number's wrong, you can't point to a formula or a line of logic  ( as it can be in a normal code) - you're just asking the model to explain itself, which it will happily do whether or not that explanation is true.
- It's a genuinely unreliable calculator. There's academic benchmarking on this - one study found that as company size increased, LLMs got *more* likely to hallucinate financial figures like , even though the real data was freely available. 

This isn't theoretical. Deloitte shipped a A$440,000 report to the Australian government that used GPT-4o in its preparation, and it went out with a fabricated quote attributed to a federal court judgment plus citations to academic papers that don't exist. Nobody caught it internally - an outside academic found it after publication, by manually checking references. Air Canada was held liable in court after its support chatbot gave a customer wrong information about refund policy . In both cases the output looked professional and confident right up until someone checked it against the source.

That's the risk with letting a model touch our numbers directly: it's not that it fails loudly, it's that it fails fluently. A wrong SUM in a formula throws an obviously wrong number you'll probably notice. A wrong SUM narrated in a paragraph reads exactly like a right one.

What I'd propose instead - is keeping the LLM entirely outside the compute path:

1. LLM parses messy input (inconsistent headers, free-text fields, varying file formats) into a clean structured shape.
2. Deterministic code - Python, pandas, SQL, whatever fits - does the actual calculation. This is the part that has to be exact, testable, and reproducible on demand.
3. LLM can narrate or summarise the output afterwards for a human reader, but never generates the number itself.

So practically: we let AI write the analysis script, not perform the analysis. Ask it to generate a Python/pandas script against the trading file, review the script like we'd review any other code, run it, and the output is deterministic and re-runnable by anyone, audit or otherwise. The model's value is in speed of getting to the code, not in being the engine that does the maths.

Happy to walk through this in person if useful, or put together a short reference pattern doc for the team.
